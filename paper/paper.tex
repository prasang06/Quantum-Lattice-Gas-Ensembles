\small\textbf{Abstract.}
Statistical mechanics asserts that the microcanonical, canonical, and
grand-canonical ensembles agree in the thermodynamic limit. For a system of a
handful of particles the agreement is only approximate, and how good the
approximation is becomes a question one can answer by direct computation. We
diagonalize the two-dimensional hard-core Bose--Hubbard model completely---every
eigenvalue in every particle-number sector---on square lattices of $4$, $9$, and
$16$ sites, and build all three ensembles from the one spectrum. Reading the
temperature and chemical potential off the microcanonical entropy by finite
differences turns out to be unreliable on spectra this small and this sharply
bounded, so we track equivalence instead through quantities that are smooth sums
over the spectrum: the relative energy fluctuation $\sigma_E/|\avg E|$ and the
relative particle-number fluctuation $\sigma_N/\avg N$, both of which fall as
$1/\sqrt M$ when the ensembles converge. For the free gas and for repulsion they
do fall, with fits close to that rate. Attraction is different. The number
fluctuation stays large, and for $V/t=-3$ its finite-size exponent is
positive---the canonical-to-grand mismatch \emph{grows} with the lattice rather
than shrinking, which is what a lattice gas on the verge of phase separation
should do. Sweeping $V/t$ from $-3$ to $+3$ turns this into a single monotonic
curve in the number channel. Repeating the scaling at every temperature from
$T/t=0.2$ to $10$ confines the anomaly to low temperature: at $V/t=-3$ the
exponent is positive only below $T\approx1.9\,t$, close to the $T_c\approx1.70\,t$
at which the hopping-free gas, an Ising model, phase separates. Mapping the model
onto an XXZ ferromagnet explains why $V/t=-2$, its isotropic Heisenberg point,
never turns positive, and predicts its low-temperature number fluctuation exactly.

\section{Introduction}
Write down a box of gas three different ways---fixed energy, fixed temperature,
fixed chemical potential---and thermodynamics says you get the same answer. The
statement is exact only when the number of particles runs to infinity. Everything
actually computed, measured in a trap, or simulated on a lattice has a finite
number of particles, and then the three descriptions differ. The size of that
difference, and the way it shrinks as the system grows, is the subject of this
paper.

We work with the hard-core Bose--Hubbard model on a square lattice: bosons that
hop between neighboring sites with amplitude $t$, repel or attract neighbors with
energy $V$, and are forbidden from sharing a site. It is a quantum lattice gas in
the plain sense of the phrase---a gas of quantum particles sitting on a
lattice---and it is realized by cold bosonic atoms in an optical lattice in the
strongly interacting limit. (It has nothing to do with the ``quantum lattice-gas
automaton,'' a cellular-automaton algorithm that shares the name.) Two features
make it a good test bed. The local Hilbert space is two states per site, so the
whole spectrum is reachable by exact diagonalization for small lattices, and the
interaction can be dialed from repulsive through free to attractive without
changing anything else.

The plan is ordinary but the order matters: build the Hamiltonian sector by
sector, check it against anything that can be computed another way, diagonalize,
and only then assemble ensembles and compare. One result of that discipline is
negative and worth stating early. The textbook route to the microcanonical
temperature---differentiate $S=\ln\Omega$ with respect to energy---does not
survive contact with a spectrum of a few thousand discrete, bounded levels. We
show why below and route around it.

\section{Model}
On an $L\times L$ lattice with $M=L^2$ sites,
\begin{equation}
\hat H=-t\!\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\!\big(\bd_i \hat b_j+\bd_j \hat b_i\big)
+V\!\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\!\hat n_i \hat n_j,
\qquad \hat n_i=\bd_i\hat b_i,
\end{equation}
with the hard-core condition $\hat n_i\in\{0,1\}$, $\hat b_i^2=(\bd_i)^2=0$. The
sum is over nearest-neighbor bonds, counted once. We set $k_B=1$, measure energy
in units of $t$, and use periodic boundaries. Because $\hat H$ commutes with
$\hat N=\sum_i\hat n_i$, it is block diagonal in particle number; each block is
diagonalized on its own and the eigenvalues feed all three ensembles. In the
occupation basis the hard-core operators carry no sign between sites, so a hop
contributes exactly $-t$ and every block is a real symmetric matrix.

A basis state is a string of occupations stored as an integer, bit $i$ holding
$n_i$. The $N$-particle sector has dimension $\binom{M}{N}$, and the sectors add
up to $2^M$. The canonical and grand-canonical sums run over the whole spectrum,
not a few low-lying states, so we diagonalize each block densely rather than with
a Lanczos method. The half-filled sector sets the cost: $\binom{16}{8}=12\,870$
for $L=4$, a few minutes on a workstation. We cache the spectra once.

\section{The three ensembles}
Everything below starts from one object: the set of eigenvalues $E_{N,\alpha}$,
where $N$ labels the particle-number sector and $\alpha$ the level within it. The
three ensembles are three ways of weighting that set.

\subsection{Microcanonical}
An isolated system with $N$ particles and energy in a window $[E,E+\Delta E)$
visits each accessible level with equal probability. Counting them,
\begin{equation}
\Omega(E,N;\Delta E)=\#\{\alpha:\,E\le E_{N,\alpha}<E+\Delta E\},\qquad
S=\ln\Omega .
\end{equation}
The first law without a volume term, $dE=T\,dS+\mu\,dN$, gives temperature and
chemical potential as slopes of the entropy surface,
\begin{equation}
\frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{\!N},\qquad
\mu=-T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial N}\right)_{\!E}.
\label{eq:micro-Tmu}
\end{equation}
Both slopes have to be taken as finite differences, since $E$ is discrete and $N$
is an integer. The window width $\Delta E$ is a modeling choice that does not go
away: $\Omega$ is a histogram, and its bin size shows up in every quantity
derived from it.

\subsection{Canonical}
Put the system in weak contact with a bath and hold the total energy fixed. The
probability of the system sitting in level $\alpha$ is proportional to the number
of bath states compatible with it, $\Omega_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_\alpha)=
e^{S_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_\alpha)}$. The system energy is tiny against the
bath, so expanding to first order,
\begin{equation}
S_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_\alpha)\simeq \text{const}-\beta E_\alpha,\qquad
\beta=\frac{\partial S_{\rm bath}}{\partial E}=\frac1T,
\end{equation}
and the Boltzmann weight follows:
\begin{equation}
p_\alpha=\frac{e^{-\beta E_{N,\alpha}}}{Z_N},\qquad
Z_N(\beta)=\sum_\alpha e^{-\beta E_{N,\alpha}} .
\end{equation}
Mean energy and its fluctuation are derivatives of $\ln Z_N$:
\begin{align}
\avg E_N&=-\frac{\partial \ln Z_N}{\partial\beta}=\sum_\alpha E_\alpha p_\alpha,\\
\Var(E)&=\frac{\partial^2\ln Z_N}{\partial\beta^2}
=\avg{E^2}-\avg E^2 .
\end{align}
The heat capacity is fixed by the fluctuation, not by any separate measurement:
differentiating $\avg E$ with respect to $T$ and using $d\beta/dT=-\beta^2$,
\begin{equation}
C_V=\frac{\partial\avg E}{\partial T}=\beta^2\,\Var(E).
\label{eq:cv}
\end{equation}
The Gibbs entropy $-\sum_\alpha p_\alpha\ln p_\alpha$ collapses to
$S=\beta\avg E+\ln Z_N$, and $F_N=-T\ln Z_N=\avg E-TS$.

\subsection{Grand canonical}
Let the system trade particles with the reservoir as well. The same first-order
expansion, now in both energy and number, with $\partial S_{\rm res}/\partial
N=-\beta\mu$, gives
\begin{equation}
p_{N,\alpha}=\frac{e^{-\beta(E_{N,\alpha}-\mu N)}}{\Xi},\qquad
\Xi(\beta,\mu)=\sum_N e^{\beta\mu N}Z_N(\beta).
\end{equation}
The mean number and its fluctuation are $\mu$-derivatives of $\ln\Xi$,
\begin{equation}
\avg N=\frac1\beta\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu}=\sum_{N,\alpha}N\,p_{N,\alpha},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\avg N}{\partial\mu}=\beta\,\Var(N),
\label{eq:dndmu}
\end{equation}
the second being the compressibility sum rule, derived the same way as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv}. Since $\Var(N)>0$, $\avg N(\mu)$ increases monotonically, a
fact we lean on when matching ensembles. The mean energy is
$\avg E=-\partial_\beta\ln\Xi+\mu\avg N$.

\subsection{When they agree, and by how much}
The canonical partition function is a Laplace transform of the microcanonical
count. Writing the level density as $\rho_N(E)=\sum_\alpha\delta(E-E_\alpha)\approx
e^{S(E)}$,
\begin{equation}
Z_N(\beta)=\int\! dE\,\rho_N(E)\,e^{-\beta E}
=\int\! dE\,e^{\,S(E)-\beta E}.
\end{equation}
For large $M$ the integral is dominated by the energy $E^\ast$ where
$S'(E^\ast)=\beta$---which is exactly the microcanonical relation
Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro-Tmu} identifying the two temperatures. Expanding around the
saddle, the canonical energy distribution is Gaussian with width
$\sigma_E^2=-1/S''(E^\ast)$. Entropy and energy are both extensive, so $S'$ is
intensive and $S''\sim\mathcal O(1/M)$; hence $\sigma_E^2\sim M$ and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_E}{|\avg E|}\sim\frac{\sqrt M}{M}=\frac1{\sqrt M}.
\label{eq:relE}
\end{equation}
The same argument applied to $\Xi=\sum_N e^{\beta\mu N}Z_N$, now with a saddle in
$N$, gives $\sigma_N/\avg N\sim 1/\sqrt M$. These two ratios are the small
parameters of ensemble equivalence: when they are small the canonical energy and
grand-canonical number distributions are sharp, and fixing $T$ or $\mu$ is the
same as fixing $E$ or $N$. They are also pure spectral sums, with no
differentiation, which is what makes them usable on a small discrete spectrum.

\subsection{Why we do not read $T$ off the entropy}
Equation~\eqref{eq:micro-Tmu} is the honest definition of the microcanonical
temperature, and on a small spectrum it is close to useless. $S(E)=\ln\Omega$ is
a jagged histogram; its slope swings wildly, diverges wherever the matched energy
lands in a gap, and changes sign past the middle of the band, where the density
of states peaks and the lattice gas enters its negative-temperature regime.
Figure~\ref{fig:caloric} shows the damage. We tried surface, Gaussian-broadened,
and volume (Gibbs) entropies; none rescues a single-energy thermometer at these
sizes. So Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro-Tmu} stays in the paper as a diagnostic, with its
$\Delta E$ dependence reported, and the quantitative claims rest on
Eq.~\eqref{eq:relE} and its number-channel twin.

To match the ensembles at a common operating point we fix $N$ and $\beta$, take
the canonical $\avg E_N(\beta)$, and solve $\avg N(\beta,\mu^\ast)=N$ for the
chemical potential. Monotonicity of $\avg N(\mu)$ makes the root unique; Brent's
method finds it to $10^{-10}$. The energy per site compared across ensembles at
that point, $|\avg E_{\rm grand}-\avg E_{\rm can}|/M$, is the third metric we
track.

\subsection{Four limits we can do by hand}\label{sec:limits}
The number channel has analytic limits that bracket what the numerics should do,
and that let us separate finite-size effects from physics.

\emph{Infinite temperature.} As $\beta\to0$ at fixed filling $n=N/M$, every Fock
state carries the same weight, so the grand-canonical $N$ is binomial and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_N}{\avg N}\;\to\;\sqrt{\frac{1-n}{nM}},
\label{eq:binomial}
\end{equation}
an exact $M^{-1/2}$ law for any $t$ and $V$.

\emph{No hopping.} Writing $\hat n_i=(1+s_i)/2$ turns the $t=0$ gas into an Ising
model, $V\hat n_i\hat n_j\to\tfrac V4 s_is_j$ plus a field that vanishes at the
particle--hole symmetric point $\mu=zV/2$ ($z=4$), which is half filling. For
$V<0$ the coupling $J=|V|/4$ is ferromagnetic, and Onsager's solution of the
square lattice~\cite{onsager} places the transition at
\begin{equation}
T_c=\frac{2J}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)}\approx0.567\,|V| .
\label{eq:tc}
\end{equation}
Below $T_c$ a lattice held at $\mu=zV/2$ is either mostly full or mostly empty:
$N=M(1\pm m_0)/2$, with Yang's spontaneous magnetization
$m_0=[1-\sinh^{-4}(2J/T)]^{1/8}$~\cite{yang}. Then $\sigma_N/\avg N\to m_0$, a
value that does not shrink with $M$ at all. Phase separation shows up in this
metric as an exponent near zero, not near $-1/2$.

\emph{Hopping: the XXZ map.} With $\bd_i=\hat S^+_i$ and $\hat n_i=\hat S^z_i+\tfrac12$,
hard-core bosons are spin-$\tfrac12$ operators~\cite{matsubara} and
\begin{equation}
\hat H=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\Big[-2t\big(\hat S^x_i\hat S^x_j+\hat S^y_i\hat S^y_j\big)
+V\hat S^z_i\hat S^z_j\Big]+\text{field}.
\end{equation}
For $V<0$ this is a ferromagnet with $J_z=|V|$ and $J_{xy}=2t$. It is easy-axis,
and phase separates at finite temperature, only when $V<-2t$. For $-2t<V<0$ it is
easy-plane: a superfluid, with no phase separation. Hopping therefore pulls the
true $T_c$ below Eq.~\eqref{eq:tc} and drives it to zero at $V=-2t$.

\emph{The Heisenberg point.} At $V=-2t$ the model is exactly an SU(2)
ferromagnet, so in 2D the Mermin--Wagner theorem rules out ordering at any $T>0$~\cite{mermin}. Its ground multiplet has total spin $M/2$ and one
member in each $N$-sector. At $\mu=zV/2$ the $M+1$ members are degenerate, so as
$T\to0$ the grand ensemble is uniform on $N=0,\dots,M$ and, for even $M$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_N}{\avg N}\;\to\;\sqrt{\frac{M+2}{3M}}\;\to\;\frac1{\sqrt3}.
\label{eq:heis}
\end{equation}
This too is size independent, but it approaches its limit from above, so a fit on
small lattices returns a small \emph{negative} exponent.

\section{Validation}
Each check below is a regression test in the code.
\emph{(i)}~Every sector block is symmetric to machine precision.
\emph{(ii)}~Block dimensions are $\binom{M}{N}$ and sum to $2^M$.
\emph{(iii)}~A separately written full-space Hamiltonian is block diagonal in $N$,
and the union of per-sector spectra equals its spectrum to $10^{-10}$---particle
number is conserved.
\emph{(iv)}~For $V=0$ on $L=3,4$ the one-particle sector reproduces the
tight-binding band $-2t[\cos k_x+\cos k_y]$ to $10^{-15}$; on the $2\times2$
plaquette it gives the four-site ring $\{-2t,0,0,2t\}$.
\emph{(v)}~$\Tr\hat H$ equals the summed interaction, since hopping is off
diagonal.
\emph{(vi)}~Ground-state energies from a sparse Lanczos solver agree with the
dense spectra ($E_0/t=-10.347$ at $L=3$, $-18.000$ at $L=4$ for $V=0$).
\emph{(vii)}~$C_V=\beta^2\Var(E)$ and $\partial_\mu\avg N=\beta\Var(N)$ match
numerical derivatives of the means to $10^{-5}$.
\emph{(viii)}~The limits come out right: $S\to\ln(\text{ground degeneracy})$ and
$C_V\to0$ as $T\to0$; $S\to\ln(\dim)$ and $\avg E\to$ the spectral mean as
$T\to\infty$.
\emph{(ix)}~The limits of Sec.~\ref{sec:limits} come out of the full spectra:
Eq.~\eqref{eq:binomial} at $\beta t=10^{-4}$ for repulsion, attraction, and the
free gas, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:heis} at $V=-2t$ to $10^{-9}$ ($0.6124$ at $M=16$).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Spectrum, heat capacity, filling}
The spectrum is symmetric about zero and peaked in the middle
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dos})---a bounded band with a large zero-energy degeneracy, both
generic to bipartite hopping. The heat capacity is a single broad bump that
vanishes at both ends of the temperature range (Fig.~\ref{fig:cv}); repulsion
pushes the bump to higher $T$ and attraction to lower, tracking the change in the
characteristic energy scale. The grand-canonical filling passes through
half-filling at $\mu=0$ by particle--hole symmetry, and the $L=3,4$ curves have
already nearly collapsed onto a common shape while $L=2$ still stands apart
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fill}). Entropy per site climbs from zero at $T=0$---a
non-degenerate ground state---to the infinite-temperature ceiling
$\ln(\dim)/M$, which itself creeps up toward $\ln2$ as the lattice grows
(Fig.~\ref{fig:entropy}).

\subsection{The caloric curve, and the thermometry problem}
Figure~\ref{fig:caloric} is the clearest single picture of why the
microcanonical temperature is not to be trusted here. The canonical $T(E)$,
obtained by inverting the smooth $\avg E(T)$, rises cleanly through the lower half
of the band. The finite-difference $1/(\partial S/\partial E)$ scatters around it
below the band center and then goes negative above it. The negative values are
not a bug---a bounded spectrum genuinely has negative-temperature states---but
they make the slope useless as a thermometer at a single energy.
\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig11_caloric_curve.png}
\caption{Caloric curve: smooth canonical $T(E)$ (line) against microcanonical
finite differences (crosses), $L=4$, $N=8$, $\Delta E=0.75$.}\label{fig:caloric}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Matching the ensembles, and the finite-size trend}
Fix $N$ and $\beta$, tune $\mu^\ast$ so $\avg N=N$, and the canonical and
grand-canonical energies per site sit almost on top of each other, splitting by a
small amount at intermediate temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:eens}). That gap, and the
two fluctuation ratios, are what we follow against size.

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} and Table~\ref{tab:fits} collect the three metrics
versus $M$ for seven couplings. For the free gas and for repulsion all three fall
with size; the free-gas $\sigma_N/\avg N$ and per-site gap follow clean power laws
($R^2=1.00$) with exponents between $-0.6$ and $-0.9$, straddling the
$M^{-1/2}$ that Eq.~\eqref{eq:relE} predicts. The energy ratio $\sigma_E/|\avg E|$
is the noisiest of the three, as expected---it feels the bounded band and the
discreteness of the smallest lattice most strongly.

The attractive side behaves differently, and the difference is the main result.
Reading down the $p(\sigma_N/\avg N)$ column of Table~\ref{tab:fits}, the decay
exponent slides monotonically from $-1.10$ at $V/t=+3$, where repulsion restores
equivalence fastest, through $-0.64$ for the free gas, up to $+0.13$ at
$V/t=-3$. The sign flip is the point. At strong attraction the relative number
fluctuation reaches $0.96$ on the largest lattice and its exponent is positive,
so the canonical-to-grand mismatch \emph{grows} as the lattice grows. An
attractive lattice gas wants to clump; the grand ensemble lets the particle
number swing between the competing dilute and dense arrangements, and the
fixed-$N$ canonical ensemble cannot follow. The energy metrics keep shrinking for
every $V$, so the failure lives specifically in the particle-number channel,
which is where the instability lives too.

\subsection{The temperature axis}
Everything above sits at $\beta t=1$. Repeating the scaling fit at each of $40$
temperatures from $T/t=0.2$ to $10$ gives the exponents as functions of $T$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:texp}); at $T=t$ they reproduce Table~\ref{tab:fits}. At high
temperature every coupling heads for Eq.~\eqref{eq:binomial}: at $T=10\,t$ the
number exponent is between $-0.43$ and $-0.54$ for all seven couplings, against
$-0.49$ for the binomial law fitted on the same three lattices.

The attractive anomaly is a low-temperature effect. At $V/t=-3$ the number
exponent is positive only below $T\approx1.9\,t$, peaking at $+0.14$ near
$T=1.15\,t$, and on $16$ sites $\sigma_N/\avg N$ climbs to $1.00$ as $T\to0$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tnum}). The temperature where the sign changes falls close to the
classical $T_c=1.70\,t$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tc}, and Yang's $m_0(T=t)=0.994$ is close
to the $0.962$ we measure. Below the crossover the smaller lattices are rounded
further below $m_0$ than the larger ones, so the ratio \emph{rises} toward its
size-independent limit and the fit returns a positive exponent. At the lowest
temperatures all three lattices saturate and the exponent drops back toward zero
($+0.02$ at $T=0.2\,t$), as the Ising picture says it should.

$V/t=-2$ never turns positive. Its exponent flattens at about $-0.1$ below
$T\approx t$, and its number fluctuation on $16$ sites levels off at $0.612$,
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:heis} to nine digits. This is the Heisenberg point, the
boundary of the anomaly. The classical Ising curves overstate the $V/t=-1$ and
$-2$ fluctuations badly (Fig.~\ref{fig:tnum}), because at those couplings hopping
is as strong as the attraction and the classical limit does not apply.

The free and repulsive gases converge at every temperature above $T\approx0.5\,t$,
with exponents between $-0.43$ and $-0.70$ for $V/t=-1,0,+1,+2$, and steeper for
$V/t=+3$, which approaches a gapped checkerboard solid at half filling. The map
in Fig.~\ref{fig:tnum} puts the zero contour on the attractive side next to the
classical $T_c$ line, and nowhere else above $T\approx0.3\,t$.

\subsection{What to distrust}
Three sizes make for soft exponents, and the smallest point does the most to tilt
a fit. It also happens to be the oddest lattice: the $L=2$ torus de-duplicates to
a single-bonded four-site ring instead of the true double-bonded two-torus, with
a one-particle band $\{-2t,0,0,2t\}$ rather than the $-4t$ bandwidth minimum. The
$L=3$ torus has its own quirk---an odd period makes it non-bipartite, so it loses
the $E\leftrightarrow-E$ spectral symmetry that $L=2$ and $L=4$ keep, though the
$N\leftrightarrow M-N$ symmetry that pins $\avg N=M/2$ at $\mu=0$ survives at
every size. The $L=2,3,4$ sequence is therefore three somewhat different
geometries stitched together, and some of the scatter in Table~\ref{tab:fits} is
that stitching rather than physics. An open-boundary sequence, bipartite at every
size, or an even-$L$ series would be cleaner at the price of stronger edges.
The stitching is visible even with no physics in it: the binomial law of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:binomial}, fitted on $M=4$ and $9$ alone (where $N=4$, off half
filling), gives an exponent of $-0.36$, not $-0.5$.

The temperature sweep adds two caveats. Below $T\approx0.3\,t$ the $V/t=+1$ and
$+2$ number exponents turn positive, to $+0.6$; the fluctuations there are a few
percent at most, each lattice's finite-size gap suppresses them by a different
amount, and the odd $L=3$ torus frustrates the checkerboard order that repulsion
favors. This is a finite-size artifact, not an instability. Second,
$\sigma_E/|\avg E|$ divides by an energy that passes through zero for the
repulsive gas near $T\approx2$--$3\,t$, which produces the spikes in
Fig.~\ref{fig:texp}; $\sigma_E/M$ would avoid this.

\section{Discussion}
The choice to measure equivalence through fluctuations rather than through a
matched temperature is not a workaround; it is the content of the saddle-point
argument in Sec.~III\,D. Equivalence \emph{is} the statement that the Laplace
transform relating the ensembles is dominated by a sharp saddle, and the width of
that saddle is exactly $\sigma_E/|\avg E|$. The quantity we can compute cleanly is
the quantity that was physically at issue all along.

The attractive anomaly reads naturally as the finite-size shadow of a first-order
transition. Phase coexistence is the standard place where ensemble equivalence
breaks even in the thermodynamic limit, because the microcanonical entropy loses
concavity and the Maxwell construction separates the ensembles. We do not have a
transition on $16$ sites, but we have its precursor: a number distribution broad
enough that fixing $\mu$ and fixing $N$ stop being interchangeable, and broadening
rather than sharpening as the lattice grows.

The temperature sweep and the limits of Sec.~\ref{sec:limits} make that reading
concrete. The anomaly sits where the Ising map puts phase separation, below a
temperature close to the classical $T_c$, and only on the easy-axis side of the
XXZ map, $V<-2t$. It also clarifies what the signature is. Phase separation
does not make the number fluctuation grow without bound; it makes it stop
shrinking, an exponent near zero, and on small lattices the approach to that
plateau can come from below (a positive fitted exponent, $V/t=-3$) or from above
(a slightly negative one, $V/t=-2$). The sign alone is not the diagnostic. What
separates the attractive side from the rest is that its exponent sits far from
$-1/2$ at low temperature, where the free and repulsive exponents do not.

What would sharpen the picture now is more sizes, on lattices that share one
geometry. The exponents are fitted on three lattices of three different shapes.
Going to $L=5$ would not fix this, since a $5\times5$ torus is as non-bipartite
as $3\times3$; tilted square clusters of $8$, $10$, $16$, $18$, and $20$ sites are
all bipartite, and with translation symmetry the largest needs blocks of only
about $9\,000$ states.

\section{Conclusion}
Built from one spectrum, the three ensembles of a small quantum lattice gas agree
to a degree that improves with size for repulsive and non-interacting particles
and fails to improve---indeed worsens---for strongly attractive ones, the failure
confined to the particle-number channel where the gas would rather phase separate.
Getting there required recognizing that the microcanonical thermometer breaks on
small bounded spectra and measuring equivalence through its fluctuations instead,
which is both what works numerically and what the saddle-point picture says to
measure. A temperature sweep places the failure below the phase-separation
temperature of the hopping-free gas and on the easy-axis side of the XXZ map,
$V<-2t$, and the high-temperature and Heisenberg-point limits are reproduced
exactly.
